01Concept

A hobby project succeeds by staying interesting, not by finding users.

Why this exists

The phone question

Your S25 has better GNSS hardware than this board. Dual-band L1+L5, assisted fixes over cellular in seconds where the L76K takes thirty from cold, multi-constellation fusion, and years of vendor tuning behind it. Any plan that ignores that is dishonest, so deal with it first: if the thing you want to track is yourself, do not build this. Install a logging app and get better data in five minutes.

The case for dedicated hardware begins exactly where the phone stops.

Two of those rows do the real work. The first is ownership of the asset — tracking means the device is on the thing and you are not, and a phone in your pocket cannot tell you your bike left the compound while you slept. The second is the operating system: Android will not let you sample location every ten seconds for a week. Doze, app standby and One UI’s own battery management will throttle or kill you, silently, and no permission turns that off. A dedicated device has no OS with opinions about your background work.

The third is quieter but matters for everything in section 13: Android hands you a fused, filtered Location object. It will not give you per-satellite SNR, a nanosecond-accurate 1PPS edge, or a guaranteed 200 Hz IMU stream. Half the interesting extensions need the layer underneath the one Android exposes.

What this is, and what it is not

This is a hobby build. That is not a hedge — it is a design constraint, and a stricter one than a product brief. A product has to be finished; a hobby project has to stay interesting, and it is allowed to end the moment it stops being so. Everything below is scoped by that.

Because it is a hobby build…Consequence
Nobody depends on itNo OTA, no enclosure, no field hardening, no unattended deployment. Roughly a third of a product roadmap, deleted.
The learning is the pointKeep the parts that teach: multi-client arbitration, generated wire formats, an embedded web stack, a real debugger.
Motivation is the scarce resourceReorder for the earliest visible milestone. Three phones on a shared map in week two, not week seven.

Permission granted. You do not need a use case. “I want to understand how this class of system works” is a complete reason, and it is the honest one here. If a trekking group or a riding club later wants it, good — but do not let a hypothetical user dictate the build order of something you are making for yourself.

Logger, tracker, or something with an opinion

A logger records and waits to be asked. A tracker notices things and tells you. The gap between them is entirely firmware, and it's where all the interesting work lives — the device has to decide, without connectivity, whether something is worth reporting.

Design commitments

  • Offline is the default state, not the error state. Every feature must work with the phone in another country. Connectivity only makes things faster, never possible.
  • The device owns policy, the app owns presentation. Geofences are evaluated on the device. The app draws them and pushes them down; it never evaluates them itself for live decisions.
  • One protocol implementation. Written once in Kotlin, consumed by the app, the CLI, and the test harness. The firmware conforms to a spec, not to whatever the app happens to send.
  • Everything runs on hardware you control. Not an ideological position — a practical one. A design whose sync path depends on a hosted service has a failure mode you cannot debug and a lifespan you do not set.

What the hard parts actually are

Reading GPS and printing coordinates is an afternoon. The engineering that makes this worth three months sits in four places, and none of them are the sensor.

  • Protocol design. BLE hands you a stream of notifications with no framing, no ordering guarantee across reconnects, and no resume. Everything above that you build: sequence numbers, CRC, application-level flow control, resume-from-offset.
  • Ownership boundaries. Deciding which side is authoritative about what. This is the root cause of nearly every sync bug in real products, and getting it right once teaches you to spot it everywhere.
  • Time reconstruction. The device has no trustworthy clock without a fix, so the phone has to repair the timeline afterwards from a monotonic counter and the fixes on either side.
  • Constrained storage. Fixed-width records, ring buffers, and the reason parsing is the enemy of seeking.

By the end you can point a sniffer at any commercial BLE gadget and recognise what its designers did, and where they cut corners.

What makes this one different

Framing, 2026-07-25 — a modern walkie-talkie. Encrypted messaging for whoever is inside the building, or within radio range of the node. Walkie-talkie is the metaphor — group, range-bound, immediate, no dialling and no accounts — not voice: this stays text. Range is enforced by physics rather than policy, which is the honest version of a geofence: reach the node and you are in the group; walk out and you are not. Each user's messages live in a Room (SQLite) database on their own phone; the node relays and holds only what is undelivered, as ciphertext it cannot read.

Group messaging without infrastructure is a solved problem: phones can mesh over BLE among themselves, relay for each other, and need nothing bought. If chat alone were the goal, there would be no reason to build this — and no reason to buy a board.

So the node has to earn its place, and it earns it by being the one thing a pile of phones cannot be: a piece of infrastructure you carry. It stays awake when the phones sleep, it remembers when they forget, it holds a radio none of them have, and it knows the time and its own position from the sky rather than from a network. Everything below follows from that, and each pillar is something a phone-only mesh structurally cannot do.

The node logs every fix with a monotonic sequence number, serves any client its exact delta, and holds sealed traffic for people who are not here yet; each phone keeps its own durable archive in Room. The three features that make it itself

Most of the roadmap is table stakes. These are the ones that would be missed if they were cut — the reason this is a specific thing rather than a generic tracker:

  • The airtime economy, made visible. One radio, a hard 1 % duty cycle, and up to nine clients means airtime is a scarce shared resource with a price. So show the price: what a message costs in milliseconds before it is sent, what share of the hour each client has spent, and why yours is behind someone else's. Every other radio product hides this behind a spinner. Hiding it is what makes those products feel broken when the link is slow — the user is never told they are queued behind physics.
  • The encrypted dead-drop. The node holds sealed messages for people who are not here yet. Walk into range tomorrow and it hands over what was left for you, plus where everyone was while you were gone. It queues and schedules that traffic from {addressee, length} alone and never sees a body. Only possible because something stayed awake and remembered — the memory pillar turned into a feature you can name.
  • The power ladder. Runtime as a visible contract rather than a surprise: serve everything → BLE only → beacon only → sleep, each step tied to a stated battery threshold, announced to every client with the reason and an estimate from the coulomb counter (“3 h like this, 40 h if the AP drops”). A group can plan around a node that tells them what it is about to stop doing.

Encryption, and where the messages actually live

Keys come from an X25519 exchange at pairing, confirmed by a six-digit code shown on the node's own OLED — authenticated key exchange with no server, no QR and no secret typed twice, using a screen the board already has. Per-pair secrets for addressed messages; a node-issued group key, rotated when the roster changes, for the shared channel. Bodies are sealed with an AEAD, so the node can schedule and queue a message from its addressee and length alone.

The durable copy lives on the phone, in Room — message, peer, track, cursor and outbox tables in app-private storage. The node keeps a short backfill ring plus undelivered dead-drop traffic and nothing else, because the node is a relay you might switch off, reflash, or walk away from; the copy that matters belongs to the person at either end of the conversation.

What this does not protect. The node cannot read message bodies, but it does see who talks to whom, when, and how long the message was. Traffic analysis is not defended against, and message bodies sit decrypted in app-private storage on the phone. This is written down because a “secure” badge implying more would break the honesty rule harder than any spinner ever could. Separately: encrypting the track log at rest with an eFuse-burned key is still rejected (section 01) — that is a different threat model and it has not changed.
The rule that keeps this honest. Every one of these is a way of saying the same thing: the constraint is the interface. Duty cycle, battery, storage and range are not problems to hide from the user — they are the product's only genuinely interesting content, so they get rendered rather than smoothed over.

What you have. The T-Beam Supreme is the S3-Core v3.0 module seated in a baseboard — which is why the silkscreen reads S3-Core. The board as a range booster

A second board is the cheapest way to double useful coverage — and it is what makes the building case work at all.

The physics that matters: sub-GHz LoRa passes through concrete floors and walls that 2.4 GHz WiFi and BLE simply do not. So indoors, one node per floor: phones attach over WiFi/BLE to whichever node is nearest, and the nodes link to each other over LoRa. Outdoors it is the same trick across a ridge instead of a slab.

        phones ──WiFi/BLE──┐
                           ├── BOOSTER (floor 3) ──LoRa──┐
        phones ──WiFi/BLE──┘                             │
                                                         ├── PRIMARY ── clients, log, roster
        phones ──WiFi/BLE──── BOOSTER (basement) ──LoRa──┘
  • Exactly one hop. A booster talks to the primary and to nobody else. No booster-to-booster path means no routing table, no loop detection, and no duplicate suppression to get wrong — the three things that make mesh firmware hard.
  • The primary owns the log and the roster. Boosters are relays with their own local clients, forwarding a summary upward. One authority, exactly as section 04 requires.
  • Airtime is the ceiling, not the node count. Every relayed message is paid for twice, once per hop, against a 1 % duty cycle — so two or three boosters at conversational traffic is the honest limit. The UI has to show which cell a message crossed and what it cost, or the airtime-economy feature quietly starts lying.
  • A booster with no clients still logs GNSS and beacons, so it doubles as a coverage marker.
Supersedes the same day. An earlier note in this plan said “exactly two boards, statically paired”. The constraint that actually matters is the single hop, not the board count — a star of cells around one primary keeps the routing trivial and the airtime arithmetic explainable.

The dominant term is time-to-fix, not sleep current. Halving your sleep current buys days; halving your fix time buys weeks. That means the GNSS backup rail and the antenna are your real power features — and the AXP2101's coulomb counter lets you verify all of it empirically instead of guessing.

Debugging

The ESP32-S3 has a built-in USB-JTAG bridge — no external probe, no wiring. Plug in the USB-C cable and you get real breakpoints, watchpoints and single-stepping through OpenOCD and GDB. This is the single most underused feature of the chip; most people printf their way through a project that has a working debugger sitting idle.

Alongside that: esp_log with per-tag levels so you can turn the scheduler chatty without drowning in WiFi logs, a task watchdog that names the offending task, and a crash handler that writes the backtrace to LittleFS so a field failure is still readable after a reboot. Decode addresses with idf.py monitor, which symbolicates automatically.

Task decomposition

Single-owner rule. Exactly one task may touch the filesystem. Every other task requests reads and writes through a queue. This eliminates the entire class of corruption bugs that appear when a BLE transfer and a log write race, and it makes the sleep path trivially safe — storage_task drains its queue, flushes, and only then signals ready-to-sleep.

Storage layers

  • NVS — configuration, geofence blob, session keys, sync watermarks. Small, wear-levelled, survives OTA.
  • LittleFS on internal flash — the hot ring buffer, roughly the last 7 days. Fast, no card required, survives a missing SD.
  • microSD — cold archive. storage_task migrates sealed days from LittleFS to SD on a timer. With no card inserted the system runs unchanged on a shorter history.

Manifest

One entry per file, rewritten atomically — write manifest.tmp, then rename. It is the only structure the device updates frequently, so keep it small.

struct manifest_entry {   // 16 bytes, one per file
  uint32_t day;          // YYYYMMDD as an integer
  uint32_t record_count; // device-authoritative
  uint32_t synced_upto;  // last offset the phone acknowledged
  uint32_t reserved;
};

When free space drops below ~15%, delete the oldest file where synced_upto == record_count. If nothing qualifies, delete the oldest anyway and set a data_lost flag the app surfaces. Silent data loss is worse than a warning.

The ownership rule. The device is authoritative about what exists — which files, how many records. The phone is authoritative about what it has received — the synced offset. Neither ever infers the other’s state; it asks. Almost every duplicate-point and lost-point bug in this class of system comes from violating this one rule.

Geofence evaluation

// compiled form, pushed from the app, stored in NVS
struct zone {
  uint16_t id;
  int32_t  min_lat, min_lon, max_lat, max_lon;  // bbox prefilter
  uint16_t vertex_count;
  int32_t  vertices[];                          // e7 fixed point

// per fix: bbox reject is ~20 cycles, polygon test only on survivors
if (!in_bbox(fix, z)) continue;
state = point_in_polygon(fix, z) ? INSIDE : OUTSIDE;
if (state != z.last_state && ++z.streak >= 3 && dist_to_edge > 30_m)
    emit_geofence_event(z.id, state);

The streak counter plus the 30 m dead band is what stops a stationary device parked on a boundary from generating a hundred events overnight. Get this wrong and the feature is worse than useless.

The sync handshake

  1. Phone connects, negotiates the largest MTU it can get, subscribes to Status, Events and Data.
  2. Phone writes LIST; device replies over Data with its manifest.
  3. Phone compares against its own store and picks the first gap: START(day, offset).
  4. Device streams chunks on Data as fast as the connection interval allows.
  5. Every 32 chunks the phone writes ACK(last_good_seq); the device advances its window and continues.
  6. On a CRC failure or a gap in seq, the phone stops acking and re-issues START from its last verified offset.
  7. Transfer ends with a zero-length chunk. The phone commits, updates its store, and moves to the next gap.

Transports: pick per job, not per project

Three radios are already on the board and they are good at different things. Using one for everything is the mistake.

TransportThroughputNode drawRangeJob
BLE15–25 KB/s~2 mA avg~20 mAlways-on presence, status, alerts, background sync. The idle state.
WiFi SoftAP2–8 Mbit/s~100 mA~30 mBrought up on demand. Bulk history, map tiles, live WebSocket stream to the app, several phones at once.
LoRa~1 kbit/s110 mA in TXkmThe link out. Positions and short messages only, duty-cycle bound.

The AP is the expensive one — roughly fifty times BLE’s idle draw — so it must not be the default state. Bring it up on a button press, on a BLE command from a client, or on first LoRa alert, and drop it after a few minutes of no HTTP activity. That single rule is the difference between a device that lasts a weekend and one that lasts an afternoon.

What the AP costs you beyond power. Android flags a network with no internet route and may quietly fall back to mobile data mid-session — the app must bind its sockets to the WiFi Network object rather than assuming the route stays. And One UI's battery management will kill background work silently, which is why the app's BLE sync runs in a foreground service and BLE stays up underneath the AP.

Multi-client arbitration

This is the part that does not exist in a one-phone design, and it is where the real engineering lives. One radio, up to nine clients, all wanting to talk.

Client cap: hardware ceiling, not 3. The cap was raised from 3 to the hardware ceiling on 2026-07-23. NimBLE tops out at 9 concurrent BLE connections (CONFIG_BT_NIMBLE_MAX_CONNECTIONS, max 9); WiFi SoftAP at 10 stations (ESP_WIFI_SOFTAP_MAX_STA). Each client holds both a BLE link and a WiFi station, so the effective cap is 9; practical-stable is ~4–6 once per-client RAM and shared throughput are counted. The real bottleneck was never the socket count but LoRa airtime — 3 phones at 1 Hz already saturate the ~1 kbit/s link, so the scheduler below must generalise past N=3 and hand out decimated positions and honest queue reasons to everyone beyond the airtime budget.

Airtime scheduling

LoRa is the scarce resource. Three phones each queue a message against a link with a 1% duty-cycle budget, so the node needs a real scheduler rather than a queue:

Priority lanes   0 emergency  → pre-empts everything, ignores fairness
                  1 position   → aggregated, decimated, one packet per window
                  2 message    → deficit round-robin across clients
                  3 bulk       → only when the budget is otherwise idle

Per-client budget  each client gets 1/N of the remaining airtime after
                  lane 0 and 1; unused allocation decays rather than banks
                  so an absent client cannot hoard and then flood

Admission        reject at enqueue with a reason the client can render,
                  never silently drop — the user must see "queued, 40 s"

Deficit round-robin is the right primitive: each client accumulates credit in airtime milliseconds, spends it to transmit, and a chatty user cannot starve a quiet one. Roughly 200 lines of firmware, and a genuine distributed-systems problem.

Shared state, several readers

Every client needs the same message history and position set, but they attach and detach at different times. The node is the single authority; each client holds a cursor and pulls deltas from it.

struct client {
  uint8_t  id;            // 0..8, assigned at pairing
  uint32_t msg_cursor;    // last message seq delivered
  uint32_t pos_cursor;    // last position seq delivered
  uint32_t airtime_credit;// deficit round-robin, in ms
  uint8_t  key[32];       // per-client, from pairing ECDH
  transport_t active;      // BLE | WIFI | NONE
};

A client returning after an hour needs 200 messages, and must not block the two that are live. Serve backlog in bounded chunks interleaved with live traffic — the same fairness idea one layer up.

Backpressure

Three phones streaming position at 1 Hz will saturate the LoRa link instantly. The node aggregates them into one packet per window, decimates by distance rather than time, and drops points that add no information. It needs a policy about what matters, not just a pipe.

Degradation

A fourth phone tries to join: refuse with a clear reason, do not queue silently. Battery hits 15% with three people depending on the node: drop the AP, keep BLE and LoRa, tell everyone why. These are product decisions a single-user device never faces, and they are worth designing rather than discovering.

LoRa beacon — 18 bytes @ 866 MHz

Deliberately tiny. At SF10/125 kHz on 866 MHz this is roughly 370 ms of airtime, so a 1% duty cycle budget leaves enormous headroom. AES-CCM encrypted with a pre-shared key so a passive listener learns nothing. Enforce the duty-cycle ceiling in lora_task as a hard limit, not a policy — a bug should not be able to turn your tracker into a jammer.

device_id  u16     lat_e7  i32     lon_e7  i32
epoch      u32     flags   u16     battery u8   hdop u8
// then 4-byte CCM tag, transmitted as 22 bytes on air
Version everything. Byte 0 of every log file and every BLE session carries a protocol version. You will change the record layout, and a phone holding six months of history needs to know which parser applies.

Navigation and copy rules

  • Five tabs, flat. Diagnostics is reached by long-pressing the app bar title, not by a sixth tab — it's for you, not for a user.
  • One native Android app; screens are Compose so the same design system carries every tab. The wireframes below are the contract regardless of client tech.
  • Every position carries its uncertainty. Error ellipses sized from HDOP, interpolated segments dashed, stale positions labelled with their age. Never a crisp dot implying precision the GNSS did not deliver.
  • Queue state is visible. “Queued 40 s, bravo ahead of you” is honest and calming; a spinner is neither.
  • Every failure state names the failure and offers the next action. Retry from last good point, never a red triangle.
  • Config changes are staged locally and written explicitly. A tracker that silently reconfigures itself mid-edit is a tracker you can't trust.
  • Empty states are invitations. An empty Map says No trips recorded yet with a Sync action, not "No data".

One native Android app. The PWA plan was removed 2026-07-20.

Clients

Decision changed — 2026-07-20. This section originally specified a PWA served from the node (Vite/Svelte/Workbox, 300 KB budget) as the primary client. That plan is removed: background BLE sync with the screen off — a foreground service backfilling positions and chat while the phone sits in a pocket — is the feature a browser can never provide, and it outweighs the zero-install pitch for a personal build serving a known group. The superseding entry lives in PROJECT.md §2. A browser client may return later as an optional extra, never as the primary.

The Android app is the client

Kotlin end to end: the app, the protocol/codec module, and the CLI all share one implementation. Live data over a WebSocket when the SoftAP is up — with the socket explicitly bound to the WiFi Network so Android's mobile-data fallback cannot steal the session — and BLE GATT always-on underneath for presence and background sync.

Build a mock node on day one. A ~200-line script that serves the same WebSocket protocol and replays a captured session. You then develop the entire app with the hardware unplugged and iterate in seconds instead of a flash cycle. BLE cannot be mocked this way — develop the GATT path against the real board, everything else against the mock. It is the highest-leverage 200 lines in the project.
Agentic tooling: Android CLI + Journeys (2026-07-23). Google's agentic Android tools (developer.android.com/tools/agents) are the test/CI harness for the mock-first phases 01–02. The Android CLI gives terminal-driven, CI-friendly build/install/test — the "CI without hardware" goal. Journeys are natural-language UI validations ("open Map, confirm the accuracy ellipse renders; send a chat message, confirm it appears") run against the mock node — the concrete form of the instrumented-test row, board unplugged. BLE and the foreground-service background sync — the features the app exists for — are tested against the real board, never a Journey. A green Journeys run is not BLE coverage. Connection setup

Three calls that account for most of your throughput. The S25 supports 2M PHY properly, which roughly doubles the default rate.

gatt.requestMtu(517)
gatt.requestConnectionPriority(BluetoothGatt.CONNECTION_PRIORITY_HIGH)
gatt.setPreferredPhy(
    BluetoothDevice.PHY_LE_2M_MASK,
    BluetoothDevice.PHY_LE_2M_MASK,
    BluetoothDevice.PHY_OPTION_NO_PREFERRED
)

Expect 15–25 KB/s on this hardware rather than the 5–8 KB/s you get at 1M PHY and default priority. Surface the measured number on the Diagnostics screen — it's the most satisfying figure in the project and it tells you instantly when something regressed.

Opportunistic background sync

The best trick available on Android: a scan registered with a PendingIntent survives your process being killed. Register a filter on your service UUID at low power; when the tracker comes into range the system wakes you, and you enqueue an expedited worker that connects and syncs.

val filter = ScanFilter.Builder()
    .setServiceUuid(ParcelUuid(TRACKER_SERVICE))
    .build()

val settings = ScanSettings.Builder()
    .setScanMode(ScanSettings.SCAN_MODE_LOW_POWER)
    .setCallbackType(ScanSettings.CALLBACK_TYPE_FIRST_MATCH)
    .build()

scanner.startScan(listOf(filter), settings, pendingIntentFor<ScanReceiver>())

Manifest essentials

<uses-permission android:name="android.permission.BLUETOOTH_SCAN"
    android:usesPermissionFlags="neverForLocation" />
<uses-permission android:name="android.permission.BLUETOOTH_CONNECT" />
<uses-permission android:name="android.permission.POST_NOTIFICATIONS" />

<service android:name=".SyncService"
    android:foregroundServiceType="connectedDevice" />

The neverForLocation flag lets you skip location permission entirely — a far better first run. It's only honest if you never derive location from scan results, which you don't; position arrives over GATT after connecting.

One UI will kill your background sync. Samsung's "Deep sleeping apps" list and Adaptive Battery are more aggressive than stock Android, and they fail silently. Check isIgnoringBatteryOptimizations() at first run and ship an onboarding screen that walks the user to Settings → Battery → Background usage limits. Every serious tracker app has this screen; yours needs one too.

Timeline repair

Records logged without a fix carry a monotonic counter instead of a real timestamp. On the phone, walk each run of invalid records and interpolate against the counter — not the record index, since intervals aren't uniform once motion gating is active. A run with no valid fix on both sides can't be repaired; mark those points approximate and render them dashed rather than dropping or inventing them.

Command line tool

Kotlin/JVM, Clikt for parsing, jSerialComm for USB CDC, shipped as a fat jar or a GraalVM native image. It reuses core:protocol unchanged and supplies only a SerialTransport — around 200 lines of genuinely new code.

The protocol module is the only place where exhaustive testing is cheap. Use it.

Testing strategy

LayerMethodWhat it catches

Mock-first: the app comes up before the hardware does.

Build order

Restructured mock-first (2026-07-23). The old order opened with hardware (blink, an SSID in a WiFi list). Make it fight — and show the price

Now the interesting part. Deficit round-robin scheduling, priority lanes, admission control, backlog interleaving — hardened against real airtime timing (the mock exercised the logic; only hardware exercises the clock). Test it by writing a client that deliberately floods. This is the phase with the most transferable engineering in it.

It is also where the airtime economy becomes a feature rather than a scheduler: a cost in milliseconds shown before you send, each client's share of the hour, a queue you can act on (cancel, or promote to lane 0 and see what that costs), and the honest answer to "why is mine behind" — a name, not a spinner. Same phase because it is the same data; the difference is whether the user is allowed to see it.

two weekends · the reason to build this at all
04b

Encryption, the device store, and the dead-drop

Three things that only make sense together, and the phase where the walkie-talkie framing becomes real. Pairing and keys: X25519 at pairing, six digits on the node's OLED to confirm, per-pair secrets plus a node-issued group key for the shared channel. Room on the phone: the durable archive of this user's messages and track, so history survives the node being switched off, reflashed or left behind. The dead-drop: the node holds sealed messages for a callsign that is not here yet — plus the stretch of track they missed — and hands them over on arrival, alongside a last seen answer from its own log.

The wire additions are small: an addressee, a hold-until rule, a sealed body. Cursors, backlog and queue reasons already exist and work on ciphertext unchanged, which is the payoff for having scheduled by length rather than content all along.

two weekends · the memory pillar, made usable
05

Fix the drift

By now the hand-written codec — C on the node, Kotlin in the app — has bitten you at least once. That is when you introduce the schema and generate C and Kotlin from it, with shared golden vectors — because you will understand why it exists rather than taking my word for it.

one weekend · introduced when the pain is real, not before
06

Optional: the link out

LoRa underneath the scheduler, position aggregation, duty-cycle ceiling, BLE presence layer. Only worth doing if you have somewhere to take it where WiFi range genuinely runs out.

Two extensions belong here, both of which need the radio to exist first. The power ladder: serve everything → BLE only → beacon only → sleep, each step at a stated battery threshold, announced with its reason and a coulomb-counter estimate of how long each rung lasts. Beacon-only matters most — a node with a nearly flat cell should still say where it is, because that is when someone is looking for it. And the booster role (section 02): a second board relaying across one LoRa hop, so coverage spans a ridge outdoors or a concrete floor indoors — sub-GHz goes through slabs that 2.4 GHz does not. A star of cells around one primary, never a mesh, and every relayed message is paid for on both hops, which the UI has to show.

open-ended · optional by design

Two services that fall out of hardware already on the board. Time: the GNSS 1PPS pin makes the node a trustworthy clock with no internet and no cell, so phones take time from it — which is what makes chat timestamps and track alignment comparable across a group, and what lets records logged without a fix have their timeline repaired afterwards. The map: PMTiles served from the node over the SoftAP, so a phone that has never been to this valley still gets a basemap from the thing sitting on the table.

Keeping it alive

Hobby projects rarely die from difficulty. They die from lost context — you put it down for three weeks, come back, and cannot remember why the scheduler had that peculiar branch or which sdkconfig line fixed the reconnect bug.

  • Keep a dated build log in the repo. Plain markdown, one entry per session: what you tried, what surprised you, what you would do next. Costs nothing, and is the difference between resuming and restarting.
  • Commit the broken state. A branch called wip-scheduler-confused with a note in the log beats a clean repo you cannot re-enter.
  • Build the mock node early (see section 08). Working on the client with the hardware in a drawer is what lets you make progress on a weekday evening.
  • Stop at a milestone, not mid-refactor. Ending a session with tests green is a gift to whoever picks it up, which is you in a month.

13Future

Adjacent projects for when this one stops being the interesting problem.

Where it goes next

Second device

A second 868 MHz SX1262 node — a Heltec V3 or a bare ESP32 plus an Ebyte E22-900M, ₹800–2000 — turns the beacon into a two-way link and gives you a home base station logging to MQTT while you're away. Since you are already on 868, it also drops straight into the Meshtastic IN region if you want mesh alongside your own protocol. Highest-value addition, and the cheapest.

Sensor fusion

Log raw IMU at 200 Hz alongside GPS and implement a complementary filter, then an error-state Kalman filter, in the shared Kotlin module so you can iterate against captured data instead of reflashing. See how long you can dead-reckon with GPS disabled. A multi-week project on its own, and the mathematics transfers everywhere.

Desktop client

Compose Multiplatform on the existing modules. The protocol, the data layer, and most of the view models already work; only the transport and platform bits change.

Fleet mode

Several devices, one app, a base station aggregating beacons. This is where PostGIS finally earns its place — ST_DWithin for server-side geofences, trip segmentation across devices, real spatial queries.

Adjacent experiments the hardware already supports

  • Stratum-1 NTP server from the 1PPS output — genuinely useful home infrastructure and a hard timing problem.
  • Sub-GHz survey mode — sweep RSSI across 410–510 MHz, log against position, produce a coverage heatmap before deploying anything.
  • OOK decoder — the SX1262 does raw on-off keying, so the same board can decode your doorbell and weather station while it isn't tracking.
No rule for any of the above. These are not a backlog and they are not owed anything. If the vibration analyser sounds better than the LoRa layer one Saturday, build the vibration analyser — the hardware does not care and neither does anyone else. The only real failure mode for this project is grinding through a phase you stopped enjoying because a plan said so.

What this board can become — and what was picked

Before writing any code, the concept was re-opened and surveyed against everything already running on this board class in 2026. This section is the record of that review, merged from the former build-options.html.

Decisions carried out of the rethink

  • Concept reconfirmed: shared field node — GPS + tracker + chat, board as station, multiple phones, everyone visible on the map.
  • Chat scoped: one shared channel, text only at launch. No DMs, no channels, no media — one room mirrors one node, one group. Revisit only after Phase 03 is alive.
  • Client pivoted later the same day: the PWA plan was removed; the client is a native Android app (background BLE sync beats zero-install for a personal build). See section 08 and PROJECT.md §2. The landscape's "node-served web app" gap noted above is therefore deliberately left unclaimed.
  • Band preference sharpened: if the silkscreen turns out to read 433 MHz, that is now the preferred outcome (satellites and sondes live there for the parked option B). Still read the silkscreen before buying the whip.
  • Roadmap unchanged: Phases 01–03 were identical under every serious option, so nothing moved.
