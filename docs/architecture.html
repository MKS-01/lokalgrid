01System

Two halves and the link between them. Everything else in this document elaborates one of the three.

What this is

One node, many phones. Group chat and a shared live map for whoever is in range of the board — no dialling, no accounts, and nothing behind it. Membership is enforced by physics rather than policy, which is the honest version of a geofence: reach the node and you are in the group; walk out and you are not.
How to read this. Sections 01–04 describe the architecture, including parts not yet built; 05 State is the authority on what actually runs today. Anything designed but not written yet carries a planned pill.

Why the board earns its place

Group messaging with no infrastructure is a solved problem — phones can mesh among themselves and need nothing bought. The node has to justify itself by being what a pile of phones structurally cannot be: infrastructure you carry.

PillarWhat it meansWhy a phone mesh cannot
MemoryTwo tiers. The node logs every fix with a monotonic sequence number, serves any client its exact delta, and holds sealed traffic for people not here yet; each phone keeps its own archive.A mesh remembers only what a present device holds — nothing stays awake to hand you what you missed.
AuthorityOne place decides what exists and states its reasons. Clients own only their cursors.Peers with equal claim to truth must reconcile it: consensus, in a walking group.
RangeLicence-free LoRa measured in kilometres, from a fixed point, nobody needed in between. Sub-GHz also passes through concrete that 2.4 GHz does not.Mesh reach is crowd density. Two people either side of a ridge — or a slab — have no path.
TruthGNSS position and GNSS time straight from the constellation, uncertainty rendered on every point.A phone with no signal has neither a trustworthy clock nor a fix to share.
EnduranceA stated power ladder: services shed in a fixed order, each step announced with its reason and an estimate.Every phone guessing its own battery policy gives the group no predictable behaviour.

The three features that make it itself planned

  • The airtime economy, made visible. One radio, a hard 1 % duty cycle, nine clients: airtime is a scarce shared resource with a price, so show the price. What a message costs in milliseconds before it is sent, each client's share of the hour, and why yours is behind — a name, not a spinner.
  • The encrypted dead-drop. The node holds sealed messages for people who are not here yet, and the stretch of track they missed, and hands both over on arrival. It schedules that traffic without being able to read it.
  • The power ladder. Serve everything → BLE only → beacon only → sleep, each rung at a stated threshold with a coulomb-counter estimate. A group can plan around a node that says what it is about to stop doing.
The rule underneath all of it: the constraint is the interface. Duty cycle, battery, storage and range are not problems to hide from the user — they are the only genuinely interesting content this product has, so they get rendered rather than smoothed over.

The node. Everything is on the board already — the additions are two antennas and a cell.

The board

LilyGO T-Beam Supreme. The silkscreen reads S3-Core v3.0 and that is expected — the Supreme is the S3-Core module seated in a baseboard. Non-negotiable. The SX1262 and the microSD stay on separate SPI buses — sharing one gives corrupt writes that appear only when a beacon fires mid-write, which is a fault you will chase for a weekend. Never transmit without an antenna attached: every hardware-damage path on this board runs through the LoRa PA, and firmware must refuse TX until an assembly flag is set. 3.3 V maximum on any GPIO. Never burn the eFuse. Do not unseat the core module from the baseboard while powered.
Two I²C buses, not one. The display, barometer and magnetometer sit on i2c0 (sda 17, scl 18); the PMU and RTC on i2c1 (sda 42, scl 41). A scan of i2c0 alone finds no PMU on a board that runs from a battery, which is how the second bus was found — the firmware sweeps both at boot and names every address that answers, so a variant difference reads as a fact rather than a fault.

Power

Runtime is a firmware feature, not a battery specification. The three transports differ by two orders of magnitude, which is the entire reason BLE is the idle state and the AP is on demand.

TransportNode drawThroughputRole
BLE~2 mA avg15–25 KB/s (2M PHY)Always on. Presence, status, alerts — the idle state.
WiFi SoftAP~100 mA2–8 Mbit/sOn demand only. Bulk history, live stream, map tiles. Idle timeout enforced in firmware, not offered as a setting.
LoRa110 mA in TX~1 kbit/sThe link out. Positions and short messages, duty-cycle bound.

The power ladder

Each rung is entered at a stated battery threshold, announced to every client with its reason, and carries a coulomb-counter estimate of how long it lasts. Nothing here is a surprise to the group.

  serve everything   AP + BLE + LoRa + logging          // normal
  BLE only           AP dropped, clients keep syncing    // below ~15%
  beacon only        LoRa position only, no clients      // low — because this is when
                                                            someone is looking for it
  sleep              RTC + wake sources                  // critical
The dominant term is time-to-fix, not sleep current. Halving sleep current buys days; halving fix time buys weeks. The GNSS backup rail and a decent active antenna are the real power features — and the coulomb counter lets you verify that empirically instead of guessing.

Firmware

ESP-IDF v5.x with CMake directly — not Arduino, which hides the sleep, NimBLE and power APIs this needs, and not PlatformIO, whose IDF support lags on the S3. NimBLE for BLE, RadioLib as an IDF component for LoRa, esp_littlefs for storage, esp_http_server for HTTP and WebSocket.

Single-owner rule. Exactly one task touches the filesystem; everything else requests through a queue. This removes the entire class of corruption where a transfer races a log write, and it makes sleep entry trivially safe: drain, flush, signal ready.

The board as a range booster

A second board is the cheapest way to double useful coverage, and it is what makes the indoor case work at all. Sub-GHz LoRa passes through concrete floors and walls that 2.4 GHz WiFi and BLE do not — so indoors it is one node per floor, phones attaching over WiFi/BLE to whichever is nearest, nodes linked to each other over LoRa. Outdoors it is the same trick across a ridge.

        phones ──WiFi/BLE──┐
                           ├── BOOSTER (floor 3) ──LoRa──┐
        phones ──WiFi/BLE──┘                             │
                                                         ├── PRIMARY ── clients, log, roster
        phones ──WiFi/BLE──── BOOSTER (basement) ──LoRa──┘
  • Exactly one hop. A booster talks to the primary and to nobody else. No booster-to-booster path means no routing table, no loop detection and no duplicate suppression — the three things that make mesh firmware genuinely hard.
  • The primary owns the log and the roster. Boosters are relays with their own local clients, forwarding a summary upward. One authority, as the protocol requires.
  • Airtime is the ceiling, not the node count. Every relayed message is paid for twice, once per hop, against a 1 % duty cycle — two or three boosters at conversational traffic is the honest limit. The app must show which cell a message crossed and what it cost, or the airtime-economy feature quietly starts lying.
  • A booster with no clients still logs GNSS and beacons, so it doubles as a coverage marker.

Local store

The phone holds the copy that matters; the node is a relay you might switch off, reflash or walk away from.

Room rather than a file format or a second framework: it ships with Android and a hobby build should not carry persistence it has to think about. Bodies are stored decrypted in app-private storage — a phone-level threat is out of scope, and a passphrase nobody types would be theatre. SQLCipher stays an option if that changes.

Android behaviours to design around

  • One UI kills background work silently. “Deep sleeping apps” and Adaptive Battery stop the sync service without telling the app. Onboarding walks the user through exempting it, and the app detects and reports the symptom rather than showing a spinner — it cannot prevent it.
  • Android may abandon an AP with no internet route mid-session and fall back to mobile data. Bind the socket to the WiFi Network explicitly; BLE stays up underneath and the client resumes from its cursor.
  • Permissions: BLUETOOTH_SCAN is declared neverForLocation with BLUETOOTH_CONNECT alongside it, so finding the node never needs location — position arrives over the link after connecting, never from a scan result. ACCESS_FINE_LOCATION is requested for one thing only: the phone's own GNSS behind share my position, asked for at the tap rather than at first run, so the reason is on screen when the dialog appears. Refusing it leaves the app fully usable — the node's fix is shared instead, labelled as the node's.
  • MTU is negotiated, never assumed: chunk sizes come from the runtime value or the app truncates on some phones and not others.

UI rules

  • Every rendered position carries its uncertainty. Accuracy ring from HDOP, dashed interpolated segments, an age label on a stale fix. Never a crisp dot implying precision the GNSS did not deliver.
  • Queue state is a reason, never a spinner. “queued 56 s, bravo ahead of you” or “queued 60 s — radio duty-cycled”. A reason that omits why is a spinner with a number on it.
  • Failure states name the failure and the next action. A disabled control that does not say why is the same problem wearing a different hat.
  • Config is staged locally and written explicitly. Nothing reconfigures as you type; the node answers key by key, and refusals carry their reason.
  • Nothing synthetic is passed off as real. Mock peers are labelled ghost; the BLE row says phase 03 · needs the board rather than implying a link that does not exist.
  • Client identities are NATO callsigns — alpha, bravo, charlie — never personal names, anywhere in the app or its fixtures.
04Protocol

Where the two halves meet. Positions are binary; everything else is one JSON object per text frame, both ways.

The ownership rule

The node is authoritative about what exists. Each client is authoritative about what it has received. Neither infers the other's state — it asks. The node assigns every sequence number and knows what it has evicted; the client names its own cursor and never guesses. Almost every duplicate-or-lost-record bug in this class of system is a violation of that one sentence.

A session, start to finish

node → hello        {proto, deviceId, recordBytes, hz, mode, you:{id,name}, cap, duty,
                     posOldest, posNewest, posHeld}      // what I am, and what I hold
app  → cursor       {seq, posSeq}                        // what I already have, both streams
node → chat ×N                                           // message delta
node → config, peer ×N, roster, stats                    // current state of everything else
node → backlog      {from, to, count, lost, reason}      // what I owe you — and what aged out
node → binary ×60, backlogChunk {cursor, remaining}      // bounded chunk, then progress
       …repeats, interleaved with live traffic…
node → backlogDone  {cursor, live}                       // you are current; adopt this cursor
node → binary …                                          // live records, one per second

Backlog is served in bounded chunks — sixty records per 250 ms tick — precisely so a client returning after an hour catches up beside the ones that are live rather than blocking them. The node states what it owes before sending it, including how many records aged out of the log first, because a gap must be drawn as a gap and never smoothed into a straight line.

Airtime — one radio, nine clients

This is the engineering the project exists for. Three phones at 1 Hz already saturate ~1 kbit/s, so the scheduler is not an optimisation, it is the normal case.

0  emergency  // pre-empts everything, ignores fairness
1  position   // aggregated, decimated by distance — 50 m default, not by time
2  message    // deficit round-robin across clients
3  bulk       // only when the budget is otherwise idle
  • Per-client budget. Each client gets 1/N of what lanes 0 and 1 leave. Unused allocation decays rather than banks — an absent client must not hoard and then flood.
  • Admission control refuses at enqueue, with a renderable reason. Never a silent drop.
  • The reason shown is computed from the order the queue will actually transmit in — one service loop feeds both the plan and the transmit, so the UI cannot drift from the scheduler.
  • A reason names its constraint. “queued 60 s” alone is a spinner with a number on it; “queued 60 s — radio duty-cycled” or “bravo ahead of you” is an answer.
  • Local delivery and the link out are two different truths. Phones on one node exchange chat instantly over WiFi/BLE; only the LoRa relay is duty-cycled. Conflating them lies about local delivery.

Pairing and encryption planned

Keys come from an X25519 exchange at pairing, confirmed by a six-digit code shown on the node's own OLED — authenticated key exchange with no server, no QR and no secret typed twice, using a screen the board already has. Per-pair secrets for addressed messages; a node-issued group key, rotated when the roster changes, for the shared channel. Bodies are sealed with an AEAD, so the node schedules and queues from {addressee, length} and never needs the plaintext.

What this does not protect. The node cannot read message bodies, but it does see who talks to whom, when, and how long the message was. Traffic analysis is not defended against, and bodies sit decrypted in app-private storage on the phone. Written down because a “secure” badge implying more would break the honesty rule harder than any spinner. Encrypting the track log at rest with an eFuse-burned key is a different threat model and is not planned.

BLE transfer

Both transports run the same session, so a client is a client whichever wire it arrived on. The service is 6f6b616c-6772-6964-0000-000000000001, advertised in the scan response; …0002 carries control frames (write and notify), …0003 notifies records in the framing below. The service UUID goes in the scan response because flags, name, transmit power and a 128-bit UUID do not fit in a 31-byte advertisement.

+--------+--------+---------------------+---------+
| seq u16| len u16|    payload (N B)    |crc16 u16|
+--------+--------+---------------------+---------+

N = negotiated_mtu - 9
payload holds whole records only, never split
crc16 CCITT over seq + len + payload

Whole records per chunk wastes a few bytes and eliminates an entire reassembly bug class. Every 32 chunks the client acknowledges its last good sequence number and the node advances the window; on a CRC failure or a gap the client stops acknowledging and re-requests from its last verified offset. A zero-length chunk ends the transfer.

A BLE client joins the session when it subscribes, not when it connects. A notification to a characteristic with no subscriber has nowhere to go and is discarded, so hello, the config and the roster — all of which the session sends at join — must not be issued before the client's CCCD writes land. The node therefore waits for notifications to be enabled on both characteristics; by then the MTU is negotiated too, so the first chunk is sized from the real value rather than from 23. Losing either subscription is treated as a departure, not a degraded mode.

Next, in order

  1. Close out the board bring-up. A breakpoint in app_main over the built-in USB-JTAG, and the BME280 read so baro and tmp carry values instead of sentinels. The app's BLE path is done — verified against the node with two handsets attached at once.
  2. Finish the app-side work. Room for the track itself — the cursor survives a restart, the history does not — then two clients side by side with one deliberately flooding.
  3. Phase 04 — make it fight, and show the price. The scheduler hardened against real airtime timing, then the airtime economy as a feature: cost before sending, share of the hour, a queue you can cancel or promote. OLED status pages and the physical emergency button.
  4. Phase 04b — encryption, the device store, the dead-drop. X25519 pairing confirmed on the OLED, Room as the durable archive, and sealed messages held for people who are not here yet.
  5. Phase 05 — fix the drift. When the hand-written codecs have disagreed once, schema and codegen arrive with shared golden vectors.
  6. Phase 06 and beyond, optional. LoRa under the scheduler, the power ladder, the booster role; then time from the 1PPS pin and PMTiles served off the node.
Phase 03 is a complete project. Phones on a shared map served by the real board is a finished thing. Everything after it is optional by design, and stopping there is a success rather than an abandonment.

Open

The LoRa band is settled: 868, kept inside 865–867. Buy the whip to match; a mismatched one gives poor range that reads exactly like a firmware bug and degrades the PA over weeks. Nothing before Phase 06 transmits, so it gates only the radio work.
